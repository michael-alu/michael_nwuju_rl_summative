How the game works

The story. An agent runs a Nigerian pension fund worth ₦10 million for 60 trading days. Retirees and families must be paid: some payouts are planned retirements known well in advance, others (early withdrawals and death benefits) arrive with only 2 or 3 days' notice.

What the fund owns. Cash, government T-bills (easy to sell), shares in big banks (easy to sell, but the money arrives the next day) and shares in small companies, "small caps" (hard to sell: few people trade them, and selling a lot at once pushes the price down).

The 5 actions, one per day. Wait. Sell some T-bills. Sell some bank shares. Sell some small-cap shares (at most 20% of what trades that day). Call a big buyer, who takes a large block of small caps at a discount, but only on days one is offering.

The limits. Pension rules cap small caps at 35% of the fund (red ring) and keep bank shares between 35% and 70% (green band). Paying retirees shrinks the fund, so the small-cap share creeps up. If a limit is broken, the fund has 10 days to fix it; after that, or on day 60, shares are sold in a fire sale.

The catch. The small-cap price on the board only updates on days when at least 100,000 shares trade, so it is often out of date. The chart at the bottom shows how far the board price can drift from the real price.

Scoring. Every sale loses a little compared with the fair price (fees and pushing the price down). 1 point = ₦10,000 lost or saved. The towers are measured at starting prices, the same way the limits are.

Winning. The fund wins if it never needs a fire sale and its total selling cost stays under 125 basis points (1.25% of the fund). Otherwise it loses.
